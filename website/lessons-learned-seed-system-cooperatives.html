Seed Systems, Cooperatives and Market Value Chains: Experience and Lessons Learned

Andargachew Detebo, Agronomist and Project Manager, Institute for Sustainable Development (ISD), Addis Ababa, Ethiopia

Over 17 years in agricultural research, extension and rural development in Ethiopia, much of my work has focused on three linked areas: organizing farmers into seed producer groups and cooperatives, strengthening the Ethiopian seed system, and connecting smallholder producers to markets. This section sets out that experience and the lessons drawn from it.

I am also actively participating as a technical expert in developing the Ethiopian National Regenerative Agriculture Strategy (2026–2036), contributing field evidence from push-pull, soil health and seed system work.

1. Cooperative establishment from kebele to regional level

Sustainable cooperatives are built from the bottom up. I have supported farmer organizations at each administrative level:

  • Kebele: mobilizing smallholder farmers, including women and youth, into farmer research and seed producer groups, and supporting their transition to legally registered primary seed producer cooperatives (SPCs).
  • Woreda: working with woreda cooperative promotion and agriculture offices on registration, bylaws, leadership elections, record keeping and financial management training.
  • Zone: linking primary cooperatives to cooperative unions for collective input supply, seed quality control and bulk marketing.
  • Region: connecting cooperatives and unions with the regional Bureau of Agriculture, the regional cooperative agency, the regional seed enterprise and regional agricultural research institutes for early generation seed, certification and market access.

2. Organizing the Ethiopian seed system

  • Supporting the integrated seed sector development approach, which links the informal (farmer-saved), intermediate (cooperative) and formal (public and private enterprise) seed systems.
  • Facilitating participatory variety selection with EIAR and regional research institutes, and scaling selected varieties of triticale, food barley, finger millet, haricot bean, teff and sorghum through farmer-based seed multiplication under the BENEFIT-REALISE Programme.
  • Training seed producers on seed field management, roguing, post-harvest handling, storage and quality declared seed (QDS) standards, and coordinating field inspection with seed regulatory staff.
  • Advocating, through the push-pull project, for the inclusion of forage seed (Desmodium and Brachiaria) in the national seed system and the National Seed Sector Transformation Plan.

3. Market value chain development

  • Mapping seed and grain value chains to identify actors, bottlenecks and market opportunities from input supply to end buyers.
  • Linking cooperatives to buyers, unions, seed enterprises, NGOs and government seed distribution channels, and supporting collective marketing and business planning.
  • Promoting value addition and market-oriented production of cereals, pulses, horticultural crops and livestock feed through multi-stakeholder and innovation platforms in Hawassa, Halaba and Wolkite.

Lessons learned

  1. Start with a market, not only a variety. Seed producer groups that knew who would buy their seed before planting stayed active. Groups organized only around a new variety often dissolved after one season.
  2. Legal registration is a turning point. Moving from an informal group to a registered cooperative opens access to credit, contracts, early generation seed and government distribution channels. It needs sustained support on bylaws, governance and bookkeeping.
  3. Every administrative level has a role. Kebele groups provide the production base, woreda offices provide registration and oversight, unions provide scale, and regional bodies provide seed sources, certification and policy support. A weak link at any level holds the whole chain back.
  4. Seed quality builds trust. Consistent field inspection, QDS standards and good storage are what make cooperative seed competitive with formal-sector seed.
  5. Include women and youth from the start. Their participation in leadership and seed production improves adoption, and it makes the cooperative more likely to survive a change in leadership.
  6. Forage seed is a missing value chain. Technologies such as push-pull depend on Desmodium and Brachiaria seed that the formal system rarely supplies. Farmer-based forage seed production, backed by policy recognition, is needed to scale these practices.
  7. Innovation platforms connect the chain. Bringing farmers, cooperatives, researchers, traders and policymakers to the same table speeds up problem solving and turns field evidence into policy.
